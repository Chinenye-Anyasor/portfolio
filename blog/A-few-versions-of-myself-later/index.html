A Few Versions of Myself Later

November 17, 2026 · 9 min read
Chinenye Anyasor

This would be a long read; please bear with me.

Over the years, I have seen different variations of this quote: “You aren't lost; you're just in an uncomfortable stage of your life where your old self is gone, but your new self isn't fully born yet. You're in the midst of transformation.” At some point, I may have even posted it on my WhatsApp status. I always thought I understood its meaning until I lived and experienced it.

And perhaps, it taught me my biggest lessons this year.

I finished my university education in December 2022. While waiting for my clearance, I worked at my sister’s boutique. I was earning money, but I was anxious about my future. I did not know what to do with my life or how to turn the big dreams I had “dreamt up” in school into reality. It did not help that I studied a course that could not translate into a job or anything with any semblance of one. It’s one of those courses where you only realise you made a huge mistake studying it during your matriculation.

By 2023, it was clear I had a very real problem. I had finished clearance and still didn’t know what to do with my life. I had started applying for entry-level corporate positions, but never heard back from any of them. This was with my then caricature of a CV. I was under a lot of pressure. It felt like time for me to be somebody was quickly running out, and I was playing catch-up. That was until I saw PiggyVest’s opening for a People Operations Associate. I knew I could not apply; I did not have the requisite experience. But for the first time, I was intrigued by a career path.

I read through the job ad thoroughly and went further to read about HR in general, how to get into the field, where to take courses, and what books to read. Before I knew it, I had completed three courses, volunteered for some organisations, and was looking for my first opportunity. I had also used some LinkedIn profiles to map out what my career could look like if I stayed the course. The skills I needed to have a grasp of. The proper certifications to take. You know, the whole nine yards.

The point is, I was so sure that HR was my life’s mission, my life’s work.

By the end of 2023, I got my first HR opportunity as an HR assistant. Oh, I gave it my all and then some. After all, my life’s work deserved nothing short of the best. It got to a point where my manager, the head of HR, who was going on maternity leave, decided not to hire an experienced interim as planned because she was convinced I could handle it.

And so, for the next three months, I was the interim HR manager of an organisation with a workforce of over 400. By the end of that period, I was promoted to an HR Generalist, with rumours of a possible talent manager promotion. This was all within two years.

Life should be good, right? It was not.

Beneath all that work, I would occasionally catch myself feeling dissatisfied and asking if that was it. But I was always either too busy or too tired to give it proper thought.

By June 2025, I resigned from the job. I was thoroughly burned out. My capacity to perform struggled to keep up with the growth I was experiencing. More importantly, life had become so busy that I no longer had time for the things that brought me joy. The very things that allowed me to show up at work the way I did.

So I left, deciding to rest properly before re-entering the job market. During that quiet, dissatisfaction crept up again. Surely, what I had experienced at my last job could not be it. Shortly after, I started job hunting.

By the start of this year (2026), I had gotten a senior People Operations role that 2023 me would have been over the moon for. A good salary, real exposure to leadership, an opportunity to learn stakeholder management and work in a tech environment that offered real exposure to AI. Yet, I wasn’t excited, and I could not pinpoint why.

Anyways, I took the job. A girl has got to eat, you know. But as soon as I began, I knew I had made a terrible mistake. I was miserable. Something was off, but what exactly? I had no idea.

I found myself calling my senior colleagues to ask them what was next. Was that it? They all gave variations of plans for climbing the HR career ladder. But I think what I really wanted was permission to leave that seemingly perfect job and to understand my sudden lack of interest in HR, which was fast becoming a lack of interest in everything too.

I eventually left because I felt I was not giving my best. And I wasn’t, and still am not, the girl who doesn’t give her best. That has never been me. I still thank God for the privilege of having been able to leave.

Retrospectively, I think that was the point I let go of that version of myself and her dreams.

Then came the real anxiety. I had perpetual low energy; nothing brought me joy, and all I wanted to do was sleep. I kept reaching for anything and anyone that would make sense. The recurring question became: why was this happening to me?

I was in that state of mind for a major part of this year. I was also chronically online, watching what felt like thousands of videos on what to do when you don’t know what to do with your life. It felt like 2023 all over again. Only this time, it felt worse because I actually had an amazing job and had just let it go.

Anyways, watching those videos left me more confused than I already was. I desperately needed to hear my own thoughts. So, as my last act of hope, I left all social media platforms. LinkedIn included. I downloaded Substack, just to read and write. Those were the only things that made sense.

After some time, I realised that even though I may not have known exactly what I wanted to do, I knew it had to do with writing. It was the one constant in my life, from keeping journals in secondary school and through university to growing my LinkedIn account to over 13,000 followers, just by writing about my experiences as I learnt HR.

I tried ghostwriting, built a portfolio and set up a whole system, but it never went beyond that. Then I tried proposal and grant writing. It also did not go anywhere, due to my lack of interest, I presume.

You must know that all my life, my effort has always been in the direction of my interest. Always. It was and still is my litmus test for the things I want out of life.

Well, I soldiered on until the day I had a conversation with ChatGPT about technical writing. I can't remember where I heard about it first, but I remember having an insatiable appetite for reading anything and everything related to the field.

Before I knew it, I was done with The Insider’s Guide to Technical Writing by Krista Van Laan. An incredible book, as an aside. It led me to join the Write the Docs Slack community for tech writers. Soon, I had completed a three-course specialisation on Coursera.

I had also written a four-part user guide on getting started on a stock trading app called Meritrade, including FAQs and a style guide, as my unofficial portfolio samples. All written with Markdown in VS Code.

What's funny is that I can’t remember the exact day or time when my world started to feel right again. When I started feeling like me. When everything seemed to be in its place and I stopped feeling confused about life. I genuinely cannot remember.

It's almost like I woke up one day and found all this evidence of what I had been pouring my energy into for the past couple of months.

Clearly, I am not the same girl from January. That girl is long gone, and that's fine because I really like her replacement. Not just because she swapped dreams, but because of the changes she has made in other aspects of her life too.

So, my takeaways?

Firstly, I now know enough to understand that just because someone explains their experience to me in a language we both speak and understand, whether verbally or in a written format like a quote, does not mean I truly understand it. There is a certain level of empathy or perspective I can never have until the experience is a shared one.

Secondly, there was no way I could have known that I was between versions of myself. We only recognise it retrospectively, after we have settled into a new version of ourselves. It's beyond our control. I suspect I have a few more versions of myself to get through. So, in the future, I will pay attention, allow myself to go through it, feel my feelings like my friend would always advise, and not always try to make sense of things.

Also, while writing that last sentence, I realised I had experienced this earlier in life, when my first relationship ended, along with everything I thought I wanted from one.

I think the lesson life has been desperately trying to teach me is that I am allowed to change my mind. About people, beliefs, ideas and things. I am allowed to outgrow relationships, convictions, ambitions and even things I once worked incredibly hard to become. Changing my mind does not mean I was wrong to have wanted those things in the first place. And it certainly isn't evidence that something is wrong with me.

I think I've finally accepted that.

And I think you should too.
